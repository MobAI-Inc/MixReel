# Audience

Script step 2. The step list is [the script skill](../SKILL.md).

An ad holds people when it gives them something they want for their own lives, in a shape they already know. Before you choose a direction, read or build the brand's record of those longings and of what this audience already watches.

## Where it lives

The record belongs to the project's brand layer described in [project](../../mixreel/references/project.md), shared by every film for this brand.

- `brand/audience-taste.md` holds the longings broken down, the familiar material this audience knows, the reasons they keep watching, the scripts the user has approved, the ideas earlier films have used, and the approaches the user has rejected with the reason.
- `brand/taste-library/` holds one folder per analyzed example, with the video or a link, frames, and the breakdown below.
- `brand/competitors.md` holds how each competitor advertises and what its audience reacts to.

When these exist, read them and check their dates. When they are missing or empty, build them as below and show the summary to the user before writing a script from it. Corrections the user makes about the audience go into these files.

## Before you write

Name, from the brief, the reference analysis, and these files:

- the situation the viewer is in when the ad starts
- the longing the product can honestly meet
- the familiar form (demo, unboxing, before and after, founder line, screen recording, or another) you are using and why
- the point where a stranger would leave if the product has not appeared

If you cannot answer from these, ask one question. Do not fill the gap with a generic lifestyle scene, and do not invent a demographic study. Write what you settled into `brand/audience-taste.md`, so the next film starts from it.

## What a longing is

A longing is what the audience wants for their own life, usually the far side of something they live with. Someone who is ill longs less for treatment than for the ordinary days it gives back. A shy, careful person longs for a version of themselves who says exactly what they think. Pains are where to look. The longing is what the film delivers.

The kind of story, person, or setting an audience enjoys is their taste. Taste decides whether they stop at all. A film outside it looks like an ad for someone else. The longing decides what they take away. A film needs both. Write each longing in the audience's own terms and check it against this pair.

- "Gym-goers love before-and-after videos with a hard cut" is taste. "She has restarted the same plan five times, and longs to be someone who keeps a promise to herself" is a longing.

Break each longing down before using it. Say what the feeling is made of, what makes it stronger, and what makes it ring false. Then name a few moments in ordinary life where people feel it, such as a holiday dinner with family, and a few well-known scenes from films, shows, or ads this audience loves that deliver it, with why each works. The first kind is familiar to everyone. The second is loved, though not every viewer has seen it. Luck, for example, is getting something valuable, such as an object, a chance, or a friend, for almost nothing, as if chosen. Its size often shows only afterwards. Contrast sharpens it, such as others who tried hard and failed. Effort turns it into achievement, and a result stated without its worth stays flat. Winning the lottery is luck, but it is not a story. Lending an umbrella to a stranger in the rain who turns out to be the interviewer at tomorrow's job interview is a story, because the viewer works out the size of the gift herself.

These moments explain the feeling. Their plots are not copied. [Marketing idea](marketing-idea.md) borrows the mechanism of a loved scene. A film never names its longing.

An analysis at the expected depth, for Duolingo. Learners doubt two things, whether they can learn a language at all and whether they will keep going past the first week. Most stop, and stopping leaves guilt. The longing is to become someone who sticks with it and enjoys the days in between. The product meets that one longing in several places: a streak that grows each day, a short animation and chime after every correct answer, lessons arranged as levels and leagues, and an owl who nags when you skip. Each makes the next lesson feel owed and a little fun.

## Keep the approved scripts

Keep the scripts the user has approved, in full, with what the user said about each, and the approaches the user has rejected with the reason. A new film is written in the register of the approved ones and checked against the rejected ones. What earlier films used is in `brand/films.md`, described in [project](../../mixreel/references/project.md#record-every-film).

## Gather evidence

Start with what the user supplies, such as their own list of longings, audience research, earlier campaign results, and examples they admire. Add evidence the available tools can reach.

- The brand's own recent films, which set its register, and ads in the same category that have run for weeks in public ad libraries, weak evidence that the advertiser keeps paying. Running time cannot rank ads started the same day or show which element worked, and reused footage counts once.
- Popular posts this audience makes or shares, including how they use the product, and the language in their comments.
- Songs, sounds, and formats currently circulating among them.

Competitor ads show what the category already says, and rarely the longings it has missed. When no source is reachable, ask the user for examples or access instead of inventing longings from a demographic label. Record each source and its date. Taste moves quickly.

## Break each example down

For each example, write what happens on screen with times and exact text, then answer these questions.

- Which longing does it serve, and which pain does it start from?
- Why does this viewer keep watching? Name the moment and what it gives them, such as recognition of their own life, a laugh, wanting to know what happens next, a wish coming true, a real person's reaction, the urge to step in, or sensory pleasure.
- What familiar thing does it borrow, and what does it add that the viewer has not seen?
- How does the product enter, and what does the viewer learn it can do?

Identify songs and sounds by name, and mark a song unidentified instead of guessing. Group the examples by longing and by reason to keep watching, then note which ones the category overuses.

## Choose what to borrow

Familiar material can come from many places. These are examples, not a checklist.

- Well-known characters, franchises, and celebrities
- Songs and sounds already circulating
- Holidays, seasons, sporting events, and other moments on the calendar
- Current events and conversations online
- Situations and stereotypes the audience jokes about
- Visual styles and genre conventions they recognize at a glance
- Products and apps they already use, as a point of comparison
- Meme formats and caption patterns

Borrow something the evidence shows this audience knows, and pair it with one new element, usually the product scene the film is about. A script built only from familiar parts feels like a copy. A script with nothing familiar gives the viewer nothing to hold on to.

Some familiar material belongs to others. Record the rights position of each borrowed element. Commercial music, characters, trademarks, and real people need permission or a licensable route, such as a platform's commercial sound library or an archetype that evokes a character without copying protected features. Put that trade-off to the user instead of silently dropping a strong idea or using protected material.
