# The idea

Script step 3, after the audience. The step list is [the script skill](../SKILL.md).

Use this after the project's `brand/audience-taste.md` record, built with [audience](audience-taste.md), has been read. It covers how the idea is chosen, built, and staged. What the film may claim and how its product UI is realized belong to [ad script](ad-script.md).

## Read the approved scripts first

Before writing, read `brand/films.md`, and the approved scripts and the approaches turned down in `brand/audience-taste.md`. Ads in `brand/competitors.md` and `brand/taste-library/` show which forms this viewer already knows. Any of these may be empty. Write in the register of the approved scripts. An approved or well-performing idea may be carried onto another product, as [project](../../mixreel/references/project.md#record-every-film) describes.

## One event and its theme

Pick one idea that makes the message visible, written as an event a camera can shoot, such as a hand, a product, a screen, or a result. The idea is not a slogan glued on at the end.

Start from a moment this audience already lives, in which the product is already part of what they do, found in evidence of how real people use it, such as parents handing a phone to a restless child at a restaurant, or a jogger checking her watch at every red light. Test it by putting a competitor in the product's place. If the story still works, the product is not where the feeling turns, so find another moment before writing on. Flagging it for the user is not enough. The longings in the record explain what she wants from that moment. They do not choose it.

The moment brings its people and what they are to each other, which may be one person and her situation, as in the made-up example below, and their motive grows from that. Check every action against the same evidence. A relationship's name proves nothing. When an action exists only to make a turn, change the people or the situation.

Before any shot, write the theme in a sentence or two that a stranger with no context gets at a glance. Name the feeling the viewer should come away with, drawn from the taste and longings in the audience record, such as being looked after, or the small pride of getting something right the first time. Name the form of the film, such as a day in her life, a demo, or her life cut with the product's screen. Then give the chain of events that brings her that feeling, ending on what she feels. The product is how the feeling happens inside the chain, never the subject of the sentence. Say it plainly. A turn the viewer has to work out, such as the app had quietly saved her a seat three weeks earlier, does not survive a first viewing.

Find that chain in scenes that already won large audiences instead of inventing it. Films, series, shows, and long-running ads of every kind hold them, such as a sitcom misunderstanding, a cooking show's reveal, a sports film's last-second save, or a heist film's set piece, and many have spread widely as clips. Recall several that deliver this feeling, look at how the original was made and why so many people love it, and take the strongest as the prototype. Borrow its mechanism, such as who is small, what she overcomes, and how completely, and never its plot or names. Play it through these people's own personalities. The same high five from a shy intern and from a loud team captain are two different moments, and when every film's people come out the same, the films have copied a scene instead of borrowing a mechanism.

The prototype has to work in a short film, which must make clear who the people are to each other and what they want within a few lines and shots, then hold the viewer with quick reactions and shots that make the product look good to use. The payoff lands in one continuous scene, minutes after its cause. What came before it can be any length ago when one line, one caption, or one shot carries it, such as a caption reading two weeks before the trip. A prototype fits only if its payoff does not depend on the viewer feeling time pass, such as a slight answered the next day, a task delivered the next morning, a skill trained over weeks, or a secret kept for years. When the product itself works across time, such as a reminder that arrives later, that gap is the product's premise, and the reminder or one caption carries it.

When the film cuts between her own life and the product, the life part opens a gap or a contrast that the product's moment answers, such as an empty fridge on a late night and then the delivery at the door. A life event that is already a full drama on its own, such as losing a job, leaves the product nothing to answer.

Everything else, music, rendering style, camera, faces, and sound, serves that theme, inside what this audience enjoys, played the most direct way. How the product appears, as a place, a thing in hand, an interface following [product, copy, and UI on screen](ad-script.md#product-copy-and-ui-on-screen), or a mark on screen, follows from that moment and from what the brief and the brand record allow. Length, platform, and what the product can really do come from them too.

## Write it stretch by stretch

Work through the film the way a director does, deciding each stretch in turn by what the viewer sees in it and how it moves her toward the theme.

- Set the scene, often with empty shots and close detail, before anyone speaks.
- When the people enter, ask what the viewer now knows and whether that is enough of a hook to hold them. When it is not, hide part of it, such as a hand before a face or a reaction before its cause.
- Render the person's urgency with shots, such as her face as the clock runs down and a view over her shoulder of what she is racing, and give that feeling the shots it needs.
- Turn it, then leave something unshown. A cut away with one trace left can say more than the act.
- Let the payoff land, and when someone speaks to her there, her answer is part of it. Then stop on an image that is still a little open, such as relief and one last worry in the same frame, without a line that explains the moment.

Three feelings run through most short films. Atmosphere is the sense that something is about to happen before anything does. Pressure is worry for someone as a deadline or an obstacle closes in. Tension is something wanted right there and held back, which ends the moment it is said aloud.

Faces carry the change. Keep each person's emotion on one line of change. Dialogue is the few lines only a voice can deliver, written as [storyboard](storyboard.md#dialogue) describes.

## A made-up example

This is an invented film for an invented brand, written to show what good looks like. It is not a real campaign and not a plot to reuse. The product is a pair of noise-cancelling earbuds from a made-up brand, and the theme is a few minutes of quiet you give yourself on a loud morning.

A packed commuter train, shot wide and then close, with elbows, a crying toddler, a phone speaker playing someone else's video, the doors chiming. Close on her face, eyes shut, jaw tight. Her hand goes into her coat pocket and the case clicks open, in close-up, and the sound of the carriage drops to a hum the moment the second earbud is in. The camera stays on her and the carriage keeps moving around her, the toddler still crying without a sound. Her shoulders come down. At her stop she steps out onto a bright platform, takes one earbud out, and the noise of the station hits again. She hesitates, then puts it back in and walks out of frame. The only line is the brand's name and five words about quiet.

What the example shows. The scene is set with close detail before anything happens. The product enters as a small act she does for herself, and the sound change carries the turn without a line explaining it. The ending stops on an image that is still a little open, a choice made in the middle of noise, without a caption that says what it means.

## Record and present

Fill the idea and reasoning sections of [the script template](../assets/SCRIPT.template.md). Record:

- the theme, and the moment you borrowed it from
- the opening image
- the turn, in one action
- the last image, and the copy on it
- why this viewer would recognize the form
- what you are not showing

State an observable creative test or an open taste question, not a self-awarded score. Deliver the script with a reference sheet whenever real images exist, using frames from the brand's taste library, product photos, and screenshots, each labelled with the shot it supports and its source.

## When to pause

A user who asks for a script gets the whole script in one delivery, with the idea and the shot list together and marked as a draft. Do not stop between them. The only earlier pause is for empty brand files, as [build the brand files](../../mixreel/references/project.md#build-the-brand-files) describes. Asking for a script hands you the choice of idea for this draft. The user approves or turns it down when they read the result, and changing the idea means rewriting the shot list.

Stop at the idea only when the user asked to see ideas or alternatives first. Give more than one event, each its own event and not another adjective list, wait for their pick, then write the shot list for that one.

Either way the approval that must come before any illustration is approval of the finished script. Directions the user turned down stay turned down. Do not revive one by changing the caption.
